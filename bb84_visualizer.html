STEP 1

Alice encodes a bit

notebook cells 2–3 · encode()

For every photon Alice flips two coins: one for the bit (0 or 1) and one for the basis. The basis is the pair of directions she uses. In the + basis (called Z) a 0 is horizontal and a 1 is vertical. In the × basis (called X) a 0 is tilted 45° and a 1 is tilted 135°. In the circuit, an X gate turns 0 into 1, and an H gate moves the state into the × basis.

STEP 2

Bob measures in a basis of his own

notebook cell 4 · measure()

Bob doesn't know Alice's basis, so he guesses. To measure in the × basis he applies an H gate first, then reads the qubit.

Same basis as Alice

Bob gets Alice's bit, every time.

Different basis

The result is a coin flip: 0 or 1 at 50/50. The bit is lost.

STEP 3

Sift the key and count the errors

notebook cell 6 · qber()

Afterwards Alice and Bob say out loud which basis they used for each photon, but never the bits. They keep only the rounds where the bases matched, which is about half. Then they compare some of the kept bits. The share that disagree is the quantum bit error rate, or QBER. On a perfect line with nobody listening it is 0: our notebook measured QBER 0.0 for 200 photons. Real fiber adds about 3% of its own, which the live grid includes.

STEP 4

Eve intercepts and resends

notebook cell 5 · run_bb84(eavesdrop=True)

Eve can't copy a photon (the no-cloning theorem), so to learn the bit she has to measure it, then send Bob a new photon with what she saw. Like Bob, she has to guess the basis.

½ × ½ = 25%

Half the time Eve guesses the wrong basis and sends the wrong kind of photon. Bob's result on that photon is then a coin flip, wrong half the time.

QBER 0.247

Our notebook's result with Eve on every photon, against 0.0 without her (a perfect line). Turn on “Eve taps the fiber” above to see the jump yourself.

STEP 5

Can a careful spy hide in the noise?

Part 3 · threshold reasoning

A careful Eve listens to only some photons. Each extra 10% she intercepts adds about 2.5% to the QBER. Real lines also have some noise of their own, so a small Eve can hide inside it. Real QKD systems therefore treat every error as Eve's: they shrink the key with privacy amplification, and above about 11% they throw the key away.

What the 0.08% tells you

It is a simulator, not hardware. Its noise model is mostly about two-qubit gates, and BB84 uses none, so the result is close to a perfect line. The Quantinuum H2 emulator and the real IonQ Forte also model readout errors and should come out higher.
