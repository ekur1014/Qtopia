How BB84 works

The rules behind the stage, in five steps

BB84 is the first QKD scheme (QKD: keys sent as single photons). These are the rules the round above follows.

Step 1

Alice encodes a bit

notebook cells 2–3 · encode()

For each photon Alice flips two coins. One picks the bit, 0 or 1. The other picks a filter: + or ×.

+

The + filter (the Z basis): a 0 is a level line, a 1 is an upright line.

×

The × filter (the X basis): a 0 is tilted 45°, a 1 is tilted 135°.

In the circuit, an X gate turns 0 into 1, and an H gate moves the state into the × basis.

# the encode() you filled in
if bit == 1:
    qc.x(0)      # |0> -> |1>
if basis == 1:
    qc.h(0)      # Z basis -> X basis: |0> -> |+>, |1> -> |->
Step 2

Bob measures with a filter of his own

notebook cell 4 · measure()

Bob does not know which filter Alice used, so he guesses one.

To measure in the × basis he applies an H gate first, then reads the qubit.

Same filter as Alice

Bob gets Alice's bit, every time.

Different filter

Bob's result is a coin flip, 0 or 1 at 50/50. That bit is useless.

Step 3

Sift the key and count the errors

notebook cell 6 · qber()

After sending, Alice and Bob say out loud which filter they used for each photon, but never the bits. They keep only the rounds where the filters matched, about half.

Then they compare a sample of the kept bits. The share that disagree is the error rate, or QBER (quantum bit error rate).

Step 4

Eve intercepts and resends

notebook cell 5 · run_bb84(eavesdrop=True)

Eve cannot copy a photon. To learn its bit she has to measure it, then send Bob a new photon with what she saw. Like Bob, she has to guess the filter.

The ban on copying is the no-cloning theorem.

½ × ½ = 25%

Half the time Eve picks the wrong filter and sends the wrong kind of photon. Bob's bit is then wrong half the time.

QBER 0.247

Our notebook's result with Eve on every photon, against 0.0 without her. Turn on Eve taps the fiber above to see it.

Step 5

A careful spy and the noise of the line

Part 3 · threshold reasoning

A careful Eve grabs only some photons. Each extra 10% she grabs adds about 2.5% to the error rate.

Real lines make some errors of their own, so a small Eve can hide inside that noise. That is why real QKD systems treat every error as Eve's.

Some errors: shrink the key

Alice and Bob boil the key down to fewer bits that Eve knows nothing about (privacy amplification).

Above about 11%: throw it away

Too many errors to trust: Alice and Bob discard the whole key.

What the 0.08% tells you

It is a simulator, not hardware. Its noise model is mostly about two-qubit gates, and BB84 uses none, so the result is close to a perfect line.

The Quantinuum H2 emulator and the real IonQ Forte also model readout errors and should come out higher.
